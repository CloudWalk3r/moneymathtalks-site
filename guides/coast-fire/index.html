Coast FIRE: The Number That Lets You Stop Saving for Retirement

Intro

You need $1,000,000 to retire, but what if you could stop saving at 35? That's the idea behind Coast FIRE. You save hard early, then let growth carry your money to retirement. There's one number that tells you when you can stop. By the end, you'll know that number at 35. You'll also see what it takes to get there, and what waiting really costs. We'll find the target, then the coast number, then two ways to reach it. Everything is in today's dollars, at an assumed 7% a year after inflation. Let's start with the target.

The $1 million target

So how much do you actually need to retire on $40,000 a year? There's a simple rule of thumb for that. The FIRE part stands for financial independence, retire early. Coast FIRE is a gentler version. You don't retire early. You just stop saving for retirement early. To know when, you first need a target. A common shortcut is the 4% rule. It says you can take about 4% of your starting savings each year, adjusted for inflation. It's a rule of thumb from studies of 30-year retirements, not a guarantee. Here's the math. Say you want $40,000 a year in retirement, in today's dollars. Flip the 4% rule around, and divide that yearly amount by 4%. That's the same as multiplying it by 25. So the 4% rule puts the target at $1,000,000. That's the number your savings need to reach by retirement. We'll use 65 as the finish line. That's a big number. But you don't have to save all of it yourself. Growth can do a lot of the work, if you give it enough time. So how much do you need invested now for growth to finish the job?

The coast number

Here's what's at stake: decades of your paycheck going to retirement, or the freedom to stop early. So what's the number at 35? This is the coast number. It's the amount that, left alone, grows into your target by retirement. You don't add another dollar for retirement. You just leave it invested. The earlier you reach it, the smaller it can be, because the money has more years to compound. Time does the heavy lifting, not your paycheck. Let's run it. At 35, with 30 years to go and an assumed 7% after inflation, you need about $131,000 invested. Think of it as the target, shrunk back by 30 years of growth. Reach that, and you can stop saving for retirement. Growth does the rest. Left alone, that money is assumed to grow to $1,000,000 by 65, in today's dollars. So from 35 on, your retirement saving could be finished. That doesn't mean you stop working. It means your work only has to pay for today. But how do you get to that number by 35 in the first place?

Getting there by 35

Say you start at 25 with nothing saved. How much a month gets you to the coast number in time? This part is the hard push. For a set number of years, you save on purpose and invest every month. It's the same habit as any retirement plan, just squeezed into a shorter window. Once the window closes, the pressure is off. Your monthly budget is freed up for everything else in life. Here are the numbers. Starting at 25, that takes about $757 a month for 10 years, at the same assumed 7%. Over that decade, you'd add about $90,820 of your own money. Then you stop adding. From there, growth alone is assumed to carry the balance to $1,000,000 by 65. So 10 years of steady saving could cover a whole retirement target. After that, the money you were saving can go to your life now. But what if you're already 35, and starting from zero?

Starting at 35 instead

Maybe you didn't save in your twenties. What does the same target of $1,000,000 cost if you start at 35? Starting later isn't a failure. Plenty of people only get going in their thirties. But it changes the shape of the plan. There's no coasting phase, because there's no head start. Instead, you keep saving every month until retirement. Your own deposits do more of the work, and growth does less. Here's the math. Start from zero at 35 instead, and you need about $820 a month for 30 years. That's only a little more each month than the early plan. But you keep paying it three times as long. All in, that's about $295,000 of your own money. Compare that with about $90,820 for the person who started at 25. So waiting 10 years means putting in more than three times as much of your own money. Same target, same assumed growth. So let's go back to the number I promised at the start.

The biggest saving

At the start, I promised the one number that lets you stop saving for retirement at 35. Here it is. At 35, with 30 years to go and an assumed 7% after inflation, you need about $131,000 invested. Reach that, and your retirement saving is done. Growth is assumed to carry it to $1,000,000 by 65. But be clear on what coasting means. You still work, and your paycheck still has to pay the bills. And 7% is an assumption, not a guarantee. It's close to the stock market's long-run average after inflation, but real returns can be lower. The 4% rule is only a rule of thumb. This math also leaves out Social Security, taxes and fees. Still, the core idea holds. Money invested early does the heavy lifting.

Recap

Quick recap. A retirement of $40,000 a year needs about $1,000,000 under the 4% rule. At 35, about $131,000 invested can coast there by 65. Starting at 25, you reach it with about $757 a month for 10 years. Starting from zero at 35, it takes about $820 a month for 30 years instead.
