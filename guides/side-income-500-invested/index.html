Invest a $500 Side Income for 20 Years: What It Pays You Back

Intro

You spend a side income of $500 a month, but what do you lose by never investing it? Meet Marco. He works a side job, and every month the extra money comes in and goes straight back out. Keep that up for 20 years, and all that work leaves nothing behind. So here's the big question: what could that money pay Marco back if he invested it instead? We'll follow it for ten years, then twenty, then turn the pot into a monthly income. That last number is the one worth staying for. First, the assumptions. We assume an average return of 7% a year. Real markets swing, and returns aren't promised. Side income is taxed first, so the $500 is what's left to invest. Results are before investment taxes, fees and inflation. Now, ten years.

Ten years in

So what does $500 a month turn into after 10 years? Is that long enough to make it matter? Here's how it works. Each month, the money buys a small piece of the market instead of being spent. Each piece can grow, and then the growth can grow too. That's compounding. Early on, it's slow. Most of what you see is simply what you put in, with a thin layer of growth on top. Here's the math. Marco invests $500 a month for 10 years, at an assumed 7% a year. It grows to about $86,540. Now split that in two. He put in $60,000 of his own money. Growth adds about $26,540 on top. So after ten years, the pot is real money. But more than two thirds of it is still what Marco paid in himself. You'd expect the market to do most of that work. Instead, Marco did. Of that pot, $60,000 is his own deposits, and growth is only about $26,540. But compounding was only getting started. What happens if Marco doesn't stop at ten years, and the pot gets ten more?

Twenty years in

Here's what's at stake. If Marco keeps spending the $500, twenty years of side work leave him with nothing saved. So what do ten more years add? In the second ten years, something changes. Marco's deposits stay the same. But now the growth isn't working on a few months of deposits. It's working on a pot that already holds ten years of them, plus everything that pot has earned. A bigger pot means bigger growth, year after year. Marco's effort is the same. The result is not. Let's run it. Marco keeps investing for 20 years, at the same assumed 7%. The pot reaches about $260,000. Split it again. He put in $120,000 of his own money. Growth adds about $140,000. So this time, growth is the bigger part. It's more than everything Marco deposited. He doubled the time, but the pot is about three times as large. You'd expect twice the years to give about twice the money. Instead, it's about three times as much, around $260,000. And more than half of it is growth that Marco never had to work for. But a pot is just a number on a screen. What Marco really wants to know is simpler. What can it pay him each month?

What it pays you

Marco worked for $500 a month. If he stopped the side job after 20 years, how much could the pot pay him instead? We need a way to turn a pot into an income. A common one is the 4% rule. It's a rule of thumb, not a promise, and it comes from studies of long retirements. The idea is simple. Take out about 4% of the pot in a year, and leave the rest invested. Bad markets can still break it, so treat it as a guide. Here's the math. Take Marco's pot of $260,000, and apply the 4% rule. It can pay out about $10,400 a year. Divide that by 12 months, and it's about $867 a month. Now compare. Marco worked for $500 a month on the side. The pot can pay more than that, and he doesn't have to work for it. You'd expect a pot built from $500 a month to pay back less than $500 a month. Instead, under the 4% rule, it can pay about $867. The money ends up paying more than the job that built it. So let's go back to Marco, and to the question we started with.

The biggest saving

At the start I asked what Marco loses by spending his side income instead of investing it. Here's the answer I promised. Invest $500 a month for 20 years, at an assumed 7%, and the pot reaches about $260,000. Under the 4% rule, a rule of thumb, that pot can pay about $867 a month. That's more than the $500 he worked for. You'd think the side job is the income. It turns out the side job can build something that pays more than the job did. So what Marco loses isn't just the money he spent. It's a monthly income that could one day replace the side job, and then some. None of it is promised, and it's before inflation. But that's what the habit can be worth.

Recap

Quick recap. Ten years of $500 a month, at an assumed 7%, grows to about $86,540. Most of that is Marco's own deposits. Twenty years reaches about $260,000, and growth is the bigger part.
