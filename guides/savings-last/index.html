How Long Will Your Savings Last If You Live Off Them?

Intro

You have $100,000 and need $1,000 a month, so how long before the money stops? Meet Marcus. He has $100,000 saved. He wants to live on $1,000 a month from it. If the money never grows, it's gone in 100 months. That's a little over eight years. Then he has nothing left. So what gives Marcus more time? Is it his money growing, or taking a little less? We'll try both. The answer is bigger than most people guess. Here's how this works. Marcus takes money out once a month. We'll guess that what's left grows about 4% a year. That's a guess, not a promise. We'll skip taxes and fees. First, let's see what happens with no growth at all.

No growth at all

Say you take $1,000 every month, and the money just sits there. How long does $100,000 last? You can almost work this one out in your head. When money doesn't grow, the math is simple. Each month, what you take comes straight out. Nothing gets added back. It's like a jar of cash on a shelf. You take some out, and the jar gets lighter. That makes it a good place to start. It shows the worst case. It's how many months your savings cover with no help at all. Here's the math. Say Marcus takes $1,000 a month, and nothing grows. Then his $100,000 is gone in 100 months. $1,000 times 100 is exactly $100,000. That's a little over 8 years. For some people, that's enough. But look closer. Nothing is working for Marcus here. Every month is paid for by money he already saved. Not one dollar comes from money his savings earned. So 100 months is the least Marcus can count on. That's if his savings never earn a cent. Anything better has to come from growth, or from taking less. But money that's left alone usually earns something. So what if it grows about 4% a year?

Growing at 4%

Here's what's at stake. Marcus could run out after 100 months, or much later. So how much longer does the same $100,000 last if it grows? Here's the idea. The money Marcus hasn't taken out yet keeps earning a little. Each month, what it earns puts back part of what he took. At the start, there's the most money left. So that's when it earns the most. His savings shrink more slowly, and they last longer. You don't need big growth for this to work. Even a little adds months at the end. Let's run it. Marcus starts with $100,000. He takes $1,000 a month. What's left grows about 4% a year. Now his money lasts 122 months. With no growth, it lasted 100 months. 122 minus 100 is 22 months longer. That's almost two extra years. Where does that time come from? His savings earned about $21,840 while they waited to be spent. You'd expect 4% growth to add a few months at most. Instead, it adds 22. That's almost two years, from money that was just waiting its turn. But there's another way to make it last. Marcus controls this one every month. What if he takes a little less?

Taking $800 instead

Taking less each month sounds like a small change. So what if Marcus takes $800 a month instead of $1,000? How much longer does his $100,000 last? Taking less helps in two ways. First, less money comes out each month. Second, more money stays in. And the money that stays in keeps growing. Put those two together, and a small cut goes a long way. Your savings can last much longer than you'd think. When you live off savings, how much you take matters most. It's also the one part you get to choose. Here's the math. It's the same $100,000, with the same 4% growth. But now Marcus takes $800 a month. His money lasts 162 months. At $1,000 a month, it lasted 122 months. 162 minus 122 is 40 months longer. That's more than three extra years. And it came from one smaller number. He takes a fifth less, and his money lasts about a third longer. You'd think taking a fifth less would add a few months. Instead, it adds 40. That's more than three years. It beats what growth alone did for Marcus. So let's go back to the question from the start. Which change gives Marcus the most time?

The biggest saving

At the start, I asked what gives Marcus the most extra time. Here's the answer I promised. With no growth, $100,000 lasts 100 months. If it grows about 4% a year, it lasts 122 months. That's nice. But now he takes $800 a month instead. His money lasts 162 months. That's 40 months longer than taking $1,000. It's more than three extra years. You'd expect growth to be the big win. It isn't. How much he takes is the part Marcus controls. And it adds almost twice as much time as growth did. So before you pick an amount, try a few. Then count the months each one gives you.

Recap

Quick recap. With no growth, $1,000 a month uses up $100,000 in 100 months. If it grows about 4% a year, it lasts 122 months. Take $800 a month instead, and it lasts 162 months.
