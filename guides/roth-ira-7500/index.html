Max a Roth IRA in 2026: $625 a Month for 30 Years

Intro

You can max a Roth IRA at $625 a month, but what does tax cost you in 30 years? That's the full 2026 limit, spread over 12 months. Most of the final balance isn't money you put in. It's growth. And in a Roth, that growth can come out without a tax bill. By the end, you'll see exactly how big that tax-free part is, and what waiting ten years does to it. Here's the plan. We'll start with the 2026 limit, grow it for 30 years at an assumed 7%, then see what a late start costs. First, the limit itself.

The 2026 limit

So how much can you actually put into a Roth IRA in 2026? And what does that look like as a monthly habit? An IRA is an individual retirement account you open yourself, separate from any plan at work. A Roth IRA is the kind where you pay tax on the money first. After that, qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free, growth included. There's a cap on how much you can put in each year. A full Roth contribution also has an income limit. Earn above it, and you can put in less, or nothing at all. Here's the 2026 limit. You can put up to $7,500 into an IRA. Divide that by 12 months, and it's $625 a month. That's the number to remember, because a monthly amount is easier to plan around. If you're 50 or older, you get a catch-up. Your limit rises by $1,100, to $8,600 for the year. Here, we hold the limit flat every year, though it usually rises over time. So maxing a Roth doesn't have to be one big deposit. It's $625 a month, set up once and paid like any other bill. But what does $625 a month actually turn into if you keep it up for 30 years?

Maxed for 30 years

Here's what's at stake: most of a maxed Roth won't be money you put in. So how much does 30 years of $625 a month grow to? Money invested for decades grows on its own growth. Each year's gains start earning gains of their own. Early on, that effect is small, and the balance is mostly your deposits. Later, growth does most of the work. We'll use an assumed 7% average yearly return. Real markets swing, with good years and bad ones, so treat this as an estimate, not a promise. Here are the numbers. Put in $625 a month for 30 years, at an assumed 7%. The Roth grows to about $762,000. Now split that total. Your own deposits add up to $225,000. Everything else, about $537,000, is growth. In a regular taxable account, you'd owe tax on growth like that. In a Roth, qualified withdrawals in retirement are tax-free. So the money you put in is less than a third of the total. The bigger part is growth, and a Roth lets you keep it tax-free. But that result needs all 30 years. What happens if you start ten years later?

Ten years later

It's easy to put retirement off for a decade. So what does starting ten years late really cost you? Waiting doesn't just remove ten years of deposits. Every dollar you put in gets ten fewer years to grow. And the missing years would have been the biggest ones, when a large balance earns large gains. You can't fully catch up later either, because the yearly limit caps how much you can put in. Time is the one input the limit doesn't cap. Here's the late start. Begin ten years later, and max it for 20 years instead of 30. Same $625 a month, same assumed 7%. You end with about $326,000. Now compare that with the full 30 years. Take $762,000, subtract $326,000, and the gap is about $436,000. That's what those ten years cost. Most of that gap is growth you never got, not deposits you skipped. So the most powerful years to invest are the early ones. Each year you wait doesn't just push the finish line back. It shrinks it. Now back to the question from the start: how much of a maxed Roth comes out tax-free?

The biggest saving

The cold open asked what tax costs you on 30 years of $625 a month. Here's the answer. Max a Roth IRA for 30 years at an assumed 7%, and you put in $225,000 of your own money. Growth adds about $537,000. Together, that's about $762,000. In a Roth, qualified withdrawals of that growth are tax-free in retirement. Qualified means two things. You're at least fifty-nine and a half, and your Roth is at least five years old. Meet both, and tax costs you nothing on that growth. The largest part of your balance is money you never pay tax on when you take it out. That's the real power of the limit. Not the $7,500 itself, but the decades of growth it shelters.
