A $1,200 Car Repair on a Card: What It Really Costs

Intro

Your car will need a $1,200 repair one day, but what will the card cost you? New brakes, a water pump, a worn clutch. The bill always comes, and for a lot of drivers it goes straight on a credit card. We'll put that repair on a card and see how long it takes to clear, then see how saving a little each week gets you there for less. The extra you pay on the card is the number most people never add up. We'll price one repair three ways: on a card, saved from scratch, and half saved. The math assumes a 25% card rate, fixed payments of $60 a month, and no new spending on that card. First, the card.

On the card

So what happens when a $1,200 repair goes on a card, and you pay it down a little each month? How long until it's really gone? Car repairs feel like bad luck, but they're one of the most predictable bills you'll ever get. Tires wear out, batteries die, belts wear thin. You don't know the week, but you know it's coming. When there's no money set aside, the credit card becomes the repair fund by default. And a credit card doesn't just hold the bill. It charges you for keeping it, every month, until the last dollar is paid. Here's the math. Say the repair is $1,200, and it goes on a card at 25%. You pay a fixed $60 a month, and you don't add anything new. At that pace, it takes 27 months to clear. That's more than two years of payments for one repair. And because interest builds every month the balance sits there, you pay about $368 in interest on top. That money doesn't fix anything on the car. It's just the price of paying later. So the card turns a bill you could see coming into more than two years of payments. The car is fixed in an afternoon, but you're still paying for it long after. So what if the money were already waiting when the mechanic called? How long would it take to build?

Saving $25 a week

Here's what's at stake: the same repair, paid with money you already have, costs $1,200 and not a dollar more. So how long does it take to save that from scratch? A repair fund is just a small savings pot with one job. You set aside the same amount every week, before you can spend it, and you leave it alone until the car needs it. It works because you pay for the repair before it happens, instead of after. The weekly amount feels a lot like the payment you'd send the card later, except none of it goes to interest. Here's the math. Instead of waiting for the bill, you save $25 a week. No interest is working against you, so every dollar counts toward the repair. Week by week, the pot grows. Divide $1,200 by $25 a week, and you get 48 weeks. That's a little under a year. So in less than a year, the full repair is sitting there, ready, before the car ever asks for it. When the bill arrives, you pay it in full and move on. And you keep on saving, because the next repair is coming too. So saving first takes less than a year, while the card takes more than two. And every dollar you set aside goes to the car, not to interest. But what if you've already made a start, and some of the money is sitting there right now?

Half already saved

Plenty of drivers have a little cash put aside, just not enough. So if half the repair is already saved, how much sooner are you ready? Every dollar you already have shortens the wait. Starting with part of the money isn't falling short. It's a head start. And if the bill lands before the fund is full, use what you've saved first, so only the rest goes on the card. Then pay more than the minimum each month, and put nothing new on that card until it's cleared. A smaller balance, paid down fast, costs far less in interest. Here's the math. Say you already have $600 saved. The repair is $1,200, so take away the $600 you have, and $600 is still to save. At the same $25 a week, that $600 takes 24 weeks. That's about half a year, and half the wait of starting from zero. Every week you save is a week of interest you never pay. And if the car breaks down partway through, you'd only need to borrow part of the bill, not all of it. So even a partial fund changes the whole picture. Half saved means half the wait, and a much smaller balance if the car can't hold out that long. Which brings us back to the card, and the number I said most people never add up.

The biggest saving

At the start, I said there was one number most drivers never add up. Here it is, as promised. A $1,200 repair on a card at 25%, paid at $60 a month, takes 27 months to clear, and you pay about $368 in interest on top. That's money that buys nothing. No new parts, no extra miles, just the cost of paying later. Save $25 a week instead, and the full $1,200 is ready in 48 weeks, with nothing extra to pay. Car repairs aren't really a surprise. Only the timing is. So the cheapest way to pay for one is to start saving before it happens.

Recap

Quick recap. On the card, a $1,200 repair takes 27 months and costs about $368 in interest. Saving $25 a week gets you the full $1,200 in 48 weeks. With $600 already saved, the rest takes just 24 weeks. And if the bill comes early, pay more than the minimum and keep new spending off the card.
