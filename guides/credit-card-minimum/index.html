What Paying the Minimum on a $5,000 Card Really Costs

Intro

Before you pay the minimum again, see what $5,000 at 24% really costs you. Paying $150 a month adds up to about $3,322 in interest, and one change keeps most of it. Paying the minimum feels responsible, but it isn't cheap. In the next few minutes we'll work out exactly what it costs, test two simple changes, and find the one that saves you the most. Keep the last number in mind. We'll assume a 24% APR, the same payment every month, and no new spending on the card. Three tests: the minimum, double the payment, and just $50 more. Let's start with the minimum.

The minimum-payment trap

So what does paying $150 a month on a $5,000 balance actually get you? And how long until the card is finally clear? Here's the trap. At 24% a year, the card charges about 2% every month on whatever you still owe. So a big part of every payment never touches the balance. It just covers interest. The balance only shrinks by what's left over, and early on that isn't much. That's why the minimum feels like progress but moves so slowly. You pay every month, and the card keeps charging. Here's the maths. At 24% APR, $5,000 paid off at $150 a month takes 56 months. That's more than four and a half years of payments. And over those 56 months, you'd pay about $3,322 in interest, on top of the $5,000 you borrowed. Put simply, the interest adds up to about two thirds of what you borrowed. That's the real cost of the minimum: years of payments and thousands in interest, often for things you've already used up. The good news is that the fix doesn't need a new card or a new loan. So what happens if you simply pay twice as much each month?

Double the payment

Remember, the minimum costs you about $3,322 in interest. Now let's see how much of that you can keep with one change. Doubling the payment works because of how interest is charged. Interest is worked out on the balance that's left. Pay more, and the balance falls faster. A smaller balance means less interest next month, so even more of your payment goes to the balance. It snowballs in your favour. You're not just paying faster. You're giving the card less time to charge you. Pay $300 a month instead and it's gone in 21 months. That's under two years, instead of 56 months. And the interest drops to about $1,143. Compare that with the minimum: $3,322 minus $1,143 is $2,179. That's $2,179 you keep, just by doubling the payment. Doubling won't fit every budget. But it shows the real lever: every extra dollar you pay early saves you interest later. But what if doubling is too much? What does a smaller step do?

The extra $50 test

Not everyone can find another $150 a month. So let's test something smaller: just $50 more. The same rule works for small steps. Anything above your usual payment goes straight to the balance, so it starts cutting interest from the very next month. Fifty dollars is the kind of amount you can often find by cancelling something you don't use, or trimming one regular expense. It's small enough to keep up, and keeping it up is what makes it work. Just $50 more a month, $200 in total, clears it in 36 months. That's three years instead of 56 months. 56 minus 36 is 20 months sooner. And you'd pay about $1,321 less interest than with the minimum. Not as big as doubling, but $50 a month buys you 20 months without that payment. So even a small step changes the outcome a lot. The earlier you add it, the more it saves, because every month at the full balance costs you more interest. Now back to the question from the start: which single change keeps the most of that interest?

The biggest saving

Here's the answer. Paying $150 a month costs about $3,322 in interest. Doubling to $300 a month cuts it to about $1,143. That means you keep $2,179, about two thirds of the interest, and you're done in 21 months instead of 56. That's the one change that keeps the most. And if doubling is out of reach, $50 more still saves about $1,321. The rule behind both is the same: every extra dollar you pay early is a dollar the card can't charge interest on.

Recap

So, three tests. The minimum: 56 months and about $3,322 in interest. Double the payment: 21 months, and $2,179 kept. Just $50 more: 20 months sooner and about $1,321 saved. Same card, same balance, very different results. If you'd like the same maths for your own card, try your balance and payment with these steps.
