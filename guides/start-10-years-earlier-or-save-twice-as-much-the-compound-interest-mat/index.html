Start 10 Years Earlier or Save Twice as Much? The Compound Interest Math

Two savers, one question

Here is a question that sounds simple. Two people want to save for the long term. The first starts now and puts away 200 a month. The second waits ten years, then tries to catch up by saving twice as much, 400 a month. Both earn the same 7 percent a year, compounded monthly. Who ends up with more? Most people guess the second saver, because they put in more money overall. In this video we will run the actual numbers with a compound interest calculator, look at where the money really comes from, and see why time does more of the work than the size of each payment. The figures work the same in any currency, so you can follow along with your own.

How monthly compounding works

First, a quick reminder of what compounding means. Each month, interest is added to your balance. The next month, interest is calculated on that larger balance, so you earn interest on your interest as well as on the money you put in. In the early years this effect is tiny, because the balance is small and most of it is your own contributions. As the balance grows, the interest added each month gets bigger, until it can be larger than the payment you make yourself. That is why compound growth looks like a curve that starts almost flat and then bends upward. Keep that shape in mind, because it explains everything that follows.

Saver one: 30 years at 200 a month

Saver one puts in 200 a month for 30 years. Over that time they contribute a total of 72,000. At 7 percent a year, compounded monthly, the balance grows to about 244,000. That means the interest earned is about 172,000, which is more than twice what they paid in themselves. Look at the shape of the chart. The line is fairly flat for the first decade, then rises more and more steeply. Most of the growth arrives in the final years, because by then the balance is large, and every month's interest is calculated on a much bigger number.

Saver two: 20 years at 200 a month

Now imagine saver two simply starts ten years later with the same 200 a month, so they save for 20 years instead of 30. Their total contributions are 48,000, which is a third less than saver one. But the final balance is only about 104,000, and the interest earned is about 56,000. So cutting ten years from the start removed a third of the contributions, yet it removed more than half of the final balance. Those missing early years were not just years of payments. They were years in which every payment would have kept growing.

Saver two doubles up: 400 a month

Saver two notices the gap and decides to catch up by doubling the payment to 400 a month, still for 20 years. Now they contribute 96,000, which is 24,000 more than saver one ever put in. The final balance rises to about 208,000, with about 112,000 of that coming from interest. That is a big improvement, but it is still short of saver one's 244,000. Saver one paid in less money and still ended up with about 35,600 more. Doubling the payment could not make up for ten lost years of growth.

Why time beats payment size

Here is the reason. Doubling the payment exactly doubles the result. Over the same 20 years, 400 a month ends at twice what 200 a month ends at, no more and no less. That is a straight-line relationship. Adding years works differently. Each extra year lets everything already in the account grow again, including the growth from earlier years, so the effect multiplies instead of adding. In saver one's plan, the last ten years alone add about 140,000, far more than the first ten. The earlier money goes in, the more of those late, powerful years it gets to experience.

What the calculator cannot tell you

A few honest caveats. A steady 7 percent every single year is an assumption made to keep the comparison clean. Real returns go up and down, and some years are negative. Fees reduce growth, and inflation means a future balance buys less than the same number would today. None of this is advice about where to put your money. The point of the example is the shape of the math, which holds at any rate: money that starts earlier has more time to compound. If starting early is not possible, saving more still helps a lot, as the 400 a month plan shows. It simply cannot buy back time.

Try it with your own numbers

To recap: 200 a month for 30 years grew to about 244,000. 200 a month for 20 years grew to about 104,000. And 400 a month for 20 years grew to about 208,000, still behind the early starter. You can test your own situation with the free compound interest calculator linked in the description. A useful experiment is to keep your monthly amount fixed and change only the number of years, then keep the years fixed and change only the amount. Watching which change moves the result more is the clearest way to see compounding at work.
