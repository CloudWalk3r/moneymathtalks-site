The 100 Envelope Challenge: How $1 to $100 Adds Up to $5,050

Intro

You start at $1 and end at $5,050 after 100 days, but what does the back half cost you? That's the 100 envelope challenge, and it's a popular way to save. The rules are simple, and the first few weeks feel easy. But one number in the second half decides whether you finish. By the end, you'll know that number, how to plan for it, and where the money should go after. Here's the plan. We'll do the math on the full challenge, split it into two halves, try the weekly version, and then decide where the cash should live. Let's start with the rules.

$1 to $100

So how do envelopes holding $1 to $100 turn into a total that big? It's simpler than it looks. The challenge is a game with one rule. Each day, you pick an envelope and put in the amount written on it. You can draw them at random, so small days and big days mix together. You never have to find the whole total at once. You just show up and fill one envelope. Number 100 envelopes from 1 to 100. Each day, you put that many dollars into one of them. So envelope 7 gets $7, and the last envelope gets $100. After 100 days, every envelope is full. Add them all up, and you've saved $5,050. Spread over 100 days, that works out to $50.50 a day on average. So the average day doesn't ask you for $1. It asks for $50.50, and that's real money to plan around from day one. But the average hides something. The days aren't spread evenly, and the order you fill them matters.

The back half

Here's where the challenge gets tested. Fill the envelopes in order, and one half costs far more than the other. Which half, and by how much? The early envelopes are small, so the first stretch feels almost free. You build the habit, you watch the stack grow, and it feels like a win. Then the numbers on the envelopes keep climbing. The habit stays the same, but the price goes up. Go in order, and the first 50 envelopes total just $1,275. That's the easy half. Now look at the last 50 envelopes. Together they need $3,775. That's about three times as much money, in the same number of days. Every envelope in the back half is bigger than any envelope in the front. So if you're halfway through and feeling great, you've done the easy part. The hard part is still ahead, and it's the bigger part. So what if the daily pace is just too fast for your budget? There's a slower version.

The weekly version

Can you hit the same total without finding up to $100 every single day? You can, if you're willing to wait. The weekly version keeps every rule except one. Instead of one envelope a day, you fill one envelope a week. That gives each paycheck time to cover the bigger envelopes. It's slower, but it's much easier to fit around rent, groceries and bills. The goal stays exactly the same. Fill one envelope a week instead of one a day. The envelopes don't change, so the total doesn't either. It's still $5,050. It just takes 100 weeks, which is just under two years. The big envelopes in the back half now get a whole week each, not a single day. So if the daily version feels like a sprint, the weekly version is a long walk. Same finish line, just more time to get there. Either way, you end up with $5,050 in cash. So where should all that money actually sit?

Where the $5,050 goes

Envelopes are great for playing the game. But are they a good place to keep thousands of dollars once you're done? Cash in an envelope feels safe, because you can see it. But it earns nothing, and prices keep rising around it. A savings account that pays interest does the opposite. Your money stays easy to reach, and it earns a little along the way. You can still use the envelopes for the game itself. Say you move the $5,050 into a savings account at an assumed 4%. That's near what top high-yield accounts pay right now, but rates change. Over a year, that adds about $206. Now leave the same money in envelopes instead. Official inflation for the latest 12 months was 3.7%. At that pace, your cash loses about $187 of buying power in a year. So the same pile of money can grow a little or shrink a little. The only difference is where you put it when the challenge ends. Which brings us back to the number from the start, the one that decides whether you finish.

The biggest saving

Here's the number I promised you at the start. It's $3,775. That's what the last 50 envelopes hold when you fill them in order. The first 50 only need $1,275, so the back half asks for about three times as much. That's why the second half is the hard part. It's not a willpower problem. It's a math problem. So plan for it. Mix big and small envelopes from day one, so no stretch gets too heavy. Switch to the weekly version if the daily pace gets tight. Know the hard part is coming, and it won't catch you off guard.

Recap

So here's the whole thing. One envelope a day, from $1 up to $100. That adds up to $5,050 in 100 days. The first 50 envelopes are the easy part, and the last 50 hold $3,775. The weekly version gets you the same total in just under two years. And when you're done, a savings account beats an envelope, because cash loses ground to inflation.
