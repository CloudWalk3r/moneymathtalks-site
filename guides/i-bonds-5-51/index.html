I Bonds Now Pay 5.51%: What $10,000 Earns

Intro

You can earn 5.51% on $10,000 in I bonds, but what part do you never lose? That headline rate only lasts six months. Hidden inside it is a smaller number that never changes, and over time it may matter more. We'll split the rate in two, see what six months really earns, follow the part that lasts, and cover the rules before you buy. First, the assumptions. These rates are as of November 2026. The 5.51% holds only for the first six months, the inflation part can fall, and every figure is before tax. Now, where does 5.51% come from?

Where 5.51% comes from

Why is the rate 5.51%, and why won't it stay there? The answer is that it's really two rates. An I bond pays one rate made of two parts. The first is a fixed rate, set the day you buy. It never changes. The second part follows inflation. It resets every May and November, so it can rise, and it can fall. The two are added together, and that sum is the rate you see. Here are the numbers. Take new I bonds bought from November 2026 through April 2027. They pay 5.51% a year for their first six months. That 5.51% is made of two pieces. One is a 1.4% fixed rate. That's the highest fixed rate since 2006. The rest is the part that follows inflation. After six months, that inflation part resets, and your rate moves with it. So the 5.51% is a snapshot, not a promise. The piece you can count on is the 1.4%, because it never changes. But what does six months at 5.51% actually earn on real money?

Six months on $10,000

Here's what's at stake: cash sitting in a savings account, slowly losing ground. So what do six months in I bonds earn instead? To compare fairly, we use the same money and the same six months. A rate is quoted per year, so six months earns about half of it. We'll set the I bond against an ordinary savings account at the national average rate. Both are safe places for cash, but they pay very differently. Start with $10,000. Six months at 5.51% earns about $276. Now put the same $10,000 in a savings account. At the national average rate of 0.38%, six months earns about $19. Take $276, subtract $19, and the I bond earns $257 more, for the same six months. So for these six months, the I bond earns far more than an average savings account. But remember, the 5.51% only lasts those six months. So what happens after that, when only the fixed part is certain?

The 1.4% that lasts

Rates come and go, so what's left when the inflation part changes? And why does a 1.4% rate matter at all? Inflation raises prices over time, so a dollar buys less each year. The inflation part of an I bond is built to keep up with that. The fixed rate sits on top. It's growth above inflation, which means real buying power you gain. That's why a fixed rate that looks small can matter a lot. Here's the key number. The 1.4% fixed rate stays with the bond for its whole 30 year life, on top of inflation. It doesn't reset in May or November. Over 30 years, that fixed part alone grows $10,000 to about $15,200 in today's money. That's after inflation, measured in what the money can actually buy. So the fixed rate is the part that keeps working. Whatever inflation does, the 1.4% keeps adding real growth for as long as you hold the bond. Before you buy, though, there are rules about how much and how soon.

The rules

How much can you put in, and what happens if you need the money back early? I bonds trade some flexibility for that rate. There's a yearly limit on how much each person can buy. There's a stretch where your money is locked in. And there's a small cost for leaving early. None of this is a problem if you plan for it, so put in money you can leave alone. You can buy up to $10,000 a year per person. And you can't cash out for the first 12 months. Cash out before five years, and you lose the last three months of interest. On $10,000 at 5.51%, that's about $138. The interest is subject to federal income tax, but it's free of state and local income tax. So I bonds work best for money you won't need for at least a year. Hold them five years, and you keep every month of interest. Now back to the question from the start: which part do you never lose?
