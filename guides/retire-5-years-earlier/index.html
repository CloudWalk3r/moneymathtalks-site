Retire 5 Years Earlier: What $1,000,000 Costs Each Month

Intro

You need $555 a month to reach $1,000,000 by 65, so how early could you stop? Meet Daniel. He's 30, he's starting from zero, and he wants $1,000,000. Aim for 65, and that's 35 years of saving. Aim too early, and the monthly bill can outrun his budget. Aim too late, and he works years he never needed to. So how early can Daniel really retire? We'll price three finish lines: 65, 60 and 55. The last step holds the surprise. The example uses an assumed 7% a year. Markets swing, and returns aren't promised. The $1,000,000 is before taxes and fees, and not adjusted for inflation. Retiring earlier also means the pot must last more years.

Retiring at 65

Let's start with the standard plan. What does $1,000,000 by 65 take each month, if you start from nothing at 30? The idea is simple. You invest the same amount every month, and the money you put in starts earning on its own. Then those earnings start earning too. That's compounding. It's slow at first, and then it speeds up, because the pot keeps getting bigger. The key ingredient isn't a big deposit. It's time. And at 30, time is the one thing Daniel has plenty of. Here's the math. To reach $1,000,000 by 65, starting at 30 at an assumed 7%, Daniel would invest about $555 a month. That's 35 years of steady deposits. Add them all up, and he'd put in about $233,000 of his own money. Growth adds about $767,000. So most of the million isn't money he saved. It's money his money made, and most of it arrives in the final stretch. You'd expect a million to take close to a million of saving. Instead, Daniel puts in about $233,000, and growth adds more than three times that. Time does most of the heavy lifting. But that only works because growth gets 35 years. What happens when Daniel takes 5 of them away?

Retiring at 60

Here's what's at stake. Retiring at 60 would give Daniel 5 years of his life back. So how much more does he have to invest each month to buy them? Cutting 5 years hurts in two ways at once. First, there are fewer months of deposits, so each one has to be bigger. Second, and this is the part people miss, growth loses its best years. The final stretch is when the pot is largest and compounding is fastest. Retire earlier, and you cut those years off the end, not the slow ones at the start. Here's the math. Retire at 60 instead, and Daniel needs about $820 a month. Same assumed 7%, same million, same start at 30. That's $265 more every month, for 5 fewer years. On its own, that may not sound like much. But it's every single month, from the day he starts until the day he stops. The goal hasn't moved. Only the finish line has. That's the trade Daniel faces: about $265 more a month, in exchange for 5 years of his life back. You'd expect 5 fewer years of saving to feel lighter. Instead, every month gets heavier. So if stopping 5 years early costs $265 more a month, stopping 10 years early should cost twice that. Right?

Retiring at 55

Now Daniel pushes further. What does it take to stop at 55, a full 10 years before the usual finish line? Every 5 years you cut from the end, you cut the years when growth is biggest. The first cut took the last 5. The second cut takes the 5 before those, and now the pot has even less time to grow. So the monthly deposit has to do more of the work by itself. Fewer years, bigger deposits and less help from growth, all at once. Here's the math. Retire at 55, and it's about $1,234 a month. That's $414 more again, on top of the step from 65 to 60. Now look at what Daniel puts in himself. It's about $370,000 of his own money. That's far more than the $233,000 he'd need to retire at 65, even though he saves for fewer years. You'd expect fewer years of saving to mean less of your own money. Instead, Daniel puts in about $370,000, and growth does a smaller share of the work. Let's go back to Daniel, and to the question we started with: how early can he really retire?

The biggest saving

At the start I asked how early Daniel could retire. Here's the answer I promised. Retiring at 65 takes about $555 a month. Retiring at 60 takes about $820, which is $265 more. Retiring at 55 takes about $1,234 a month. That's $414 more again, and about $370,000 of his own money. You'd expect each 5 years to cost the same. Instead, the second 5 cost more than the first: $414 a month, not $265. The earlier the finish line, the steeper each step gets. So how early can Daniel retire? It comes down to one thing: how much he can invest every month, starting at 30.

Recap

Quick recap. Reaching $1,000,000 by 65 takes about $555 a month, and growth does most of the work. Retiring at 60 takes about $820, which is $265 more every month. Retiring at 55 takes about $1,234, which is $414 more again. Each 5 years earlier costs more than the last. If this helped you pick your own finish line, subscribe.
