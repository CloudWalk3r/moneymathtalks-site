How to Stop Paying Interest on Your Credit Card (Even With a Fee)

Intro

Your $6,000 card debt could take 26 months to pay off, so why not stop the interest? Meet Priya. She owes $6,000 on a credit card and pays $300 a month. If nothing changes, she'll be paying for more than two years. And a big chunk of every payment goes to interest. A new card offers 0% interest for 18 months. But moving her debt there costs a 3% fee. So is paying a fee to stop the interest really worth it? We'll compare both cards, then find the one payment that gets her done in time. These numbers are just an example. Her card charges 24% interest a year. The new card charges a 3% fee, then 0% for 18 months. She pays the same each month and adds no new spending. First, what her old card costs.

The card at 24%

So what does that $6,000 really cost Priya if she just keeps paying? You need this number before you can judge any offer. Here's how card interest works. Every month, the card adds interest on whatever you still owe. A 24% rate means about 2 cents a month for every dollar. Your payment covers that interest first. Only what's left pays down the debt. Early on, the interest is big, so the debt shrinks slowly. That's why card debt feels like it takes forever. So before you look at any offer, find out what staying put really costs. Here's what Priya pays if nothing changes. At 24% interest, $6,000 paid off at $300 a month takes 26 months. That's more than two years of payments. Over that time, she pays about $1,739 in interest. That's on top of the $6,000 she borrowed. So doing nothing costs her 26 months. And about $1,739 goes to the card company instead of staying in her pocket. Keep that $1,739 in mind. It's what Priya pays for changing nothing. And it's the number any new card has to beat, fee and all, before it's worth her time. So what if she moves all of that debt onto a card that charges 0%, and pays the fee?

The 0% transfer

Here's what's at stake: about $1,739 of interest, or one much smaller fee. So does the 0% card really win once you add the fee? Moving your debt like this is called a balance transfer. The new card pays off the old one, so now you owe the new card. For a set time, it charges no interest at all. The catch is a one-time fee: here, 3 cents for every dollar you move. It's added to what you owe, so you start out owing a bit more. But at 0%, every dollar you pay cuts the debt itself. That's where the saving comes from. Let's do the math. Moving $6,000 with a 3% fee costs $180. So now Priya owes $6,180. She keeps paying $300 a month, and it's gone in about 21 months, with no interest at all. Now compare the two. On the old card, she'd pay $1,739 in interest. Here, she pays just the $180 fee. Take $180 away from $1,739, and moving the debt saves her about $1,559. You'd expect the fee to wipe out the saving. Instead, Priya pays a $180 fee and skips about $1,739 of interest. And she's out of debt five months sooner. But there's a catch. At $300 a month she needs 21 months, and the 0% only lasts 18 months. What then?

Beat the 18-month promo

When the 0% deal runs out, whatever she still owes starts costing interest again. So how much does she need to pay to be done before that? Think of the end of the 0% deal as a deadline. If any debt is left when it ends, the card starts charging interest on what's left. Then part of the saving is gone. The fix is simple. Work out the payment that clears everything before the deadline. Then set it up from the very first month. That way, the deadline works for you, not against you. Here's the math. To pay off $6,180 within 18 months, Priya pays $344 a month. That's her $300, plus $44 more each month. Now it's gone in 18 months instead of 21 months. That's 3 months sooner, and nothing is left when the 0% ends. So that extra $44 a month is what protects her whole saving of about $1,559. You'd think $300 a month is enough once the interest is gone. It isn't, because the deal ends before the debt does. At $344 a month from day one, they end together. So let's go back to the question from the start. Is paying that fee really worth it?

The biggest saving

At the start, I asked if paying a fee to stop the interest really leaves Priya ahead. Here's the answer I promised. With $6,000 on a card at 24%, paying $300 a month costs about $1,739 in interest. Moving it onto a 0% card with a 3% fee costs her $180. So even after the fee, moving saves her about $1,559. You'd expect the fee to be the catch. It's actually the cheap part. The real risk is the deadline. Paying $344 a month clears it all inside the 18 months. So the fee isn't a trap. Priya just has to plan for the deadline and add no new spending to either card.

Recap

Quick recap. On the old card, $6,000 at $300 a month takes 26 months and about $1,739 in interest. With a 0% card and a 3% fee, you owe $6,180 but save about $1,559.
