What Inflation Does to $10,000 Sitting in Cash

Intro

Your $10,000 in cash could lose real value every year, but what will it buy you in 10 years? If prices rise an assumed 3% a year, the number in your account doesn't shrink. What shrinks is what it can buy. Most people feel that cash is the safe choice, and in one way it is. In another way it costs you every single year. We'll put an exact number on that cost, see what it takes to keep up, and work out when prices double. We'll assume prices rise 3% a year. That's a round number for the example, not a forecast. Three questions: what $10,000 buys after 10 years, what it takes to keep up, and when prices double. Let's start with the cash.

$10,000 after 10 years

So what does $10,000 sitting in a drawer, or in an account that pays nothing, really buy you 10 years from now? The balance won't change. Something else will. Inflation means prices creep up a little every year. Groceries, rent, insurance, a tank of fuel. When money sits still, it doesn't fall in number, but each dollar buys a bit less than it did before. One year barely shows. The trouble is that it compounds, just like interest does, only it works against you. After a decade, the gap is big enough to feel at the checkout. Here's the maths. If prices rise an assumed 3% a year, $10,000 in cash buys only about $7,441 of today's goods after 10 years. Put the other way round, the same basket of things that costs $10,000 today would cost about $13,440. So $10,000 minus $7,441 means about $2,559 of buying power is gone. You didn't spend it, lose it, or pay a fee. It simply slipped away while the money sat still. That's the hidden cost of cash. It feels safe because the number never drops, but over 10 years it quietly loses about a quarter of what it can do for you. So how much does your money need to earn just to stay level? And what does beating inflation really look like?

What keeping up takes

Here's what's at stake. Every year your savings earn less than prices rise, you get a little poorer without noticing. So what does it take to actually get ahead? The number to watch isn't the rate your account pays. It's that rate minus inflation, which is your real return. Earn less than inflation and your real return is negative, even though the balance grows. Earn the same, and you're only treading water. Only the part above inflation is real growth: money that buys more next year than it does today. That's why a savings rate always needs a second number next to it. Let's run an example. Say your savings earn an assumed 4.5% a year, while prices rise 3%. On paper you're growing 4.5%. But after inflation, you really grow only about 1.5% a year. That's the real return. Most of what you earn just keeps pace with prices. Only that last 1.5% is new buying power. And if your account paid less than 3%, you'd be falling behind every single year, even with interest coming in. So the question for any account isn't what it pays. It's what it pays after 3% inflation. That one subtraction tells you whether your money is really growing. There's one more way to see how fast inflation works, and it fits in your head. How long until prices double?

When prices double

If prices keep rising 3% a year, at some point everything costs twice what it does today. So when does that happen, and can you work it out in your head? There's a shortcut called the rule of 72. Divide 72 by a yearly rate, and you get roughly how many years it takes for something to double at that rate. It works for savings growing, debts growing, and prices rising. It isn't exact, but it's close enough for everyday decisions, and it turns a vague percentage into a date you can picture. Here's how it works with inflation. Prices rising an assumed 3% a year: 72 divided by 3 is 24. So prices double in about 24 years. Something that costs $10,000 today would cost about twice that, a little after two decades. For most people, that's well inside one working life. And the same rule tells you how fast your savings need to grow, just to keep up. That's what 3% a year really means. It sounds small, but within one working life, cash left alone ends up buying about half of what it buys today. So let's go back to that $10,000, and the number I promised at the start.

The biggest saving

At the start I asked what $10,000 in cash really buys you in 10 years. Here's the answer. At an assumed 3% a year, $10,000 buys only about $7,441 of today's goods after 10 years. That's about $2,559 of buying power gone, with nothing spent and no fee paid. The number in your account never changed. What changed is what it can do. Cash still has a job. It's the right home for an emergency fund and for money you'll need soon. But for money you won't touch for years, the real question is what it earns after inflation, not only whether the balance goes down.

Recap

Quick recap. At an assumed 3% inflation, $10,000 in cash buys about $7,441 of today's goods after 10 years. Earning 4.5% while prices rise 3% is only about 1.5% real growth.
