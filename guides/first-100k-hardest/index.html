Why Your First $100,000 Is the Hardest (and What Comes After)

Intro

You could stop investing after 7 slow years, but how much would you miss in the next 14? Meet Tina. Tina invests $1,000 a month, and it feels like it's going nowhere. Most of her balance is money she put in herself, so she's tempted to quit. You may have heard that the first six figures are the hardest. Is that true, and what would Tina walk away from by stopping? We'll follow the same deposit through three stretches of 7 years. The third looks nothing like the first, and it's the one worth staying for. Here are the assumptions. Tina's money grows at an assumed 7% a year, and her deposit stays at $1,000 a month. Real markets swing, some years are negative, and returns aren't promised. It's also before taxes, fees and inflation. Three stretches of 7 years. First, the slow part.

The first 7 years

So how far does $1,000 a month get Tina in her first 7 years? And how much of that balance is growth, not her own money? Growth is a percentage of what's already in the account. Early on, there isn't much in there. So even a good year adds a small amount. Meanwhile the deposits keep arriving, month after month, at full size. That's why the start feels like pushing a car uphill. Nearly all the movement Tina sees is her own effort. The growth is there, but it's quiet. Here's the math. Tina invests $1,000 a month at an assumed 7% a year. After 7 years, she has about $108,000, so she's into six figures. Now split that balance into two parts. Her own deposits come to $84,000. Growth adds only about $24,000. That's less than a quarter of the balance. Judged by this stretch alone, investing barely looks worth the worry. You'd expect 7 years of investing to mean the market did much of the work. Instead, Tina did most of it herself. It feels slow because it is slow. But that balance is now in the account, growing too. So what happens when the same deposits land on top of it?

Years 8 to 14

Here's what's at stake. If Tina quits now, she judges the whole plan by its slowest stretch. So what do years 8 to 14 add, with the very same $1,000 a month? Tina's deposits are the same size as before. But now they land on a balance that's already large. Growth is the same assumed percentage, just of a much bigger number. And each year's growth stays in the account, so next year's growth is figured on that too. Nothing about Tina's effort changes. What changes is how much money is already working beside her. Let's run it. Tina keeps the same $1,000 a month for 7 more years. By the end of year 14, the balance reaches about $284,000. Take away the $108,000 she started this stretch with. This time the balance grows by $176,000. And her deposits? The same $84,000 as before. So more than half of that gain is money Tina never put in. You'd expect the same deposits to add about the same amount. Instead, this stretch adds $176,000, against $108,000 for the first. Tina didn't work any harder. Her balance did. So the second stretch beats the first. But does that keep going, or does it level off? There's one more stretch to check.

Years 15 to 21

This is the stretch Tina can't picture yet. Same deposit, 7 more years. How much do years 15 to 21 add, and how much of it is growth? By now the balance is doing something new. At the assumed rate, a year's growth is larger than a whole year of Tina's deposits. Her deposits still matter, but they're no longer the main engine. Think of a snowball. Early on, you pack the snow on by hand. Later, it picks up more on every turn than you could add yourself. But only if it keeps rolling. Here's the last stretch. Tina adds the same $1,000 a month for seven more years. The balance goes from $284,000 to about $571,000. So the same $84,000 of deposits now adds $287,000. Split that as before. Take out Tina's deposits, and about $203,000 is growth. In the first 7 years, growth was about $24,000. Now it's more than 8 times that, from the same monthly deposit. So in this stretch, more than two thirds of the gain is growth. The deposits didn't change. The balance they landed on did. That's the part she can't feel in the early years. So let's go back to Tina in her slow first stretch, and to the question we started with.

The biggest saving

At the start I asked how much Tina would miss if she quit after 7 slow years. Here's the answer I promised. In her first 7 years, her deposits came with only about $24,000 of growth. In years 15 to 21, at an assumed 7%, the same $84,000 of deposits adds $287,000. And of that gain, about $203,000 is growth. If Tina cashes out at year 7, that's the stretch she never reaches. You'd expect the hard part to be finding more money to invest. It isn't. The deposit never changed. The hard part is staying through the years where nothing seems to happen. The first six figures are the hardest because you do most of the work yourself. After that, more of the work is done for you, but only if you're still there.

Recap

Quick recap, all at an assumed 7%. The first 7 years reach about $108,000, mostly Tina's own deposits. Years 8 to 14 add $176,000 from the same deposits. Years 15 to 21 add $287,000, most of it growth. The slow start isn't the plan failing. It's what the start looks like. If this made the slow years a little easier to sit through, subscribe.
