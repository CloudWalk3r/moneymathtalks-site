$50 a Month From Birth: What Your Child Could Have at 65

Intro

Your $50 a month from birth could reach $573,000 by 65, so why risk waiting? Meet Maya. Her daughter was just born, and Maya is weighing one small habit: $50 a month, every month, until her daughter turns 18. Skip it, and her daughter starts from zero as an adult and has to catch up on her own. So is that small monthly amount really worth it? We'll follow it to 18, then to 65, then race it against a late start. The last comparison isn't even close. The example assumes an average return of 7% a year and 3% inflation. Both are assumptions. Markets swing, returns aren't promised, and the numbers are before taxes and fees. We'll leave account types aside. First, birth to 18.

Birth to 18

So what does $50 a month become by the time Maya's daughter turns 18? And how much of that is money Maya actually put in? Investing a small amount every month works in two ways at once. Each deposit adds to the pile. And the pile earns a return, which then earns its own return. That second part is compounding. Early on, it's almost invisible, because the pile is mostly deposits. But the longer the money sits, the more of it comes from growth instead of from you. Starting at birth gives compounding the longest runway there is. Here's the math. Invest $50 a month from birth, at an assumed 7% a year, and by 18 it's about $21,540. Now split that in two. Over those 18 years, Maya puts in $10,800 of her own money. Growth adds about $10,740 on top. So almost half the pot is money Maya never had to save. You'd expect $50 a month to stay small money. Instead, by 18, growth has matched almost every dollar Maya put in. Her daughter has about $21,540, and Maya only puts in $10,800. But 18 is where Maya stops paying in. What happens if nobody touches that money for the next 47 years?

Left alone until 65

Here's what's at stake for Maya's daughter. Spend the pot at 18, and the head start is gone. Leave it alone, and how big could it get by 65? After 18, no more money goes in. The pot just sits there and keeps compounding on its own. Each year's growth is bigger than the last, because it grows on a bigger pile. That's why the later decades do most of the work. But there's a catch. A dollar decades from now won't buy what a dollar buys today, because prices rise. So any far-off number needs an honest translation. Here's the math. Leave the $21,540 untouched from 18 to 65, and at the same assumed 7%, it could reach about $573,000. Not one extra deposit goes in during those 47 years. Now the honest part. By then, prices will be much higher than they are today. At an assumed 3% inflation, that $573,000 is worth about $83,890 in today's money. That's the figure to keep in mind, not the headline. You'd expect the big number to be the real prize. Instead, the honest figure is about $83,890 in today's money. It's a smaller number, but it's still real growth. But what if Maya waits, and her daughter starts on her own at 25? How much would she need each month to catch up?

Starting at 25 instead

So can a late start catch up? Say her daughter skips the head start and begins at 25. What would it take to land on the same $573,000 by 65? Starting later doesn't just mean fewer years of deposits. It means fewer years of growth on every deposit. The money from birth has decades to compound before her daughter is even an adult. A late starter has to replace all of that growth with her own savings. So the monthly amount has to be much bigger, and it has to keep going for a long time. Here's the math. To reach the same $573,000 by 65, starting at 25, she'd need about $218 a month at the same assumed 7%. And she'd have to keep that up for 40 years. Add up those deposits, and that's about $105,000 of her own money. Compare that with the $10,800 Maya puts in from birth. Same finish line, very different price. You'd think starting at 25 just means saving a bit more. Instead, Maya's daughter would pay in nearly 10 times as much to end up in exactly the same place. So let's go back to Maya, and to the question we started with.

The biggest saving

At the start I asked whether $50 a month from birth is really worth it for Maya. Here's the answer I promised. On these assumptions, yes, and the reason isn't the big number at 65. It's the price of waiting. From birth, Maya puts in $10,800 over 18 years. If her daughter starts at 25 instead, reaching the same result takes about $105,000 of her own money. You'd expect a late start to cost a little more. Instead, it costs nearly 10 times as much. None of this is promised, and markets swing. But the lesson is simple: early dollars get the most years to grow. Maya's $50 a month isn't really buying money. It's buying time.

Recap

Quick recap. $50 a month from birth, at an assumed 7%, is about $21,540 by 18. Left untouched until 65, it could reach about $573,000, which is about $83,890 in today's money. And catching up from 25 takes about $218 a month, or about $105,000 of your own money. Start early, and let time do the work.
